Instructional Design Portfolio

Most of what a producer does on the first morning of a shoot is instructional design. The plan lives in a few heads; by call time it has to live in everyone's, clearly enough that a crew can act on it without stopping to ask. I spent fourteen years in independent film and television doing that: training crews on set, briefing departments, and turning schedules into call sheets people could work from against the clock.

Since 2021 I've done the same work on 20 Below, a tabletop roleplaying game. The learners are players, game masters, and the fifteen-person team building it with me. I bring new team members onto the rules, the tools and the way we work, and I run the playtests where new players learn the game at the table. Those sessions are where the design gets its real test. Every place a player stalls, asks the same question twice, or reaches for the book in the middle of a fight becomes a note for the next draft.

The case studies below come from that shipped work: the rulebook, its quick references, and the tools that teach the rules while people play. Each one covers the problem, the analysis, the design decisions and the outcome.

The Rung That Made Players Worse
Retiring a Skill tier that made low-Attribute learners worse than no training at all. Playtesters raised it and exact odds confirmed it. It came out without taking value away anywhere else, every saved character was carried across, and players got a way to rebuild.
Learner-Centred Rule Design & Iteration
The Rules You Don't Have to Remember
Performance support at the table: one Roll dice window that reads a single roll three ways, a character sheet inside the virtual tabletop, and a GM's tracker that talks to it, all on one set of rules code. Measured before and after: 36 roll buttons became 5, and two rolls became one.
Performance Support
One Page, One Question
Twenty-three quick-reference sheets built as job aids: one question per sheet, the numbers computed before anything was drawn, and every sheet kept in step with the rules. Drawing them caught four problems in the rules themselves.
Job Aids & Reference Design
Learning the Game by Building a Character
The Character Creator as a new player's first lesson: fourteen steps in the order the game needs them, what the GM granted asked first, mistakes greyed out with the reason beside them, and every choice explained where it's made.
Guided Onboarding
A Rulebook You Read vs. a Rulebook You Reach For
Separating "learning" material from "in-the-moment lookup" material to cut task time under pressure.
What the Book Doesn't Tell the Person Running It
Designing decision-support content for facilitators running live sessions — a different job than the participant-facing material.
One Spider, Checked Three Ways
Turning "the GM's call" into a rule players can learn and plan around: depth without excess crunch, tested on four benchmark learners, from Fragile Frankie to Maxed Maggie, and then made the standard for every venom.

One Chapter, Taught Two Ways
One rule written once and applied two ways, for map players and theater-of-the-mind players alike. Sequenced simple to complex, tested on five benchmark learners from Fragile Frankie to Maxed Maggie, and grounded in real-world walking data.
